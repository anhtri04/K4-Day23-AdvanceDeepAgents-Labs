# Reinforcement Learning for LLM Reasoning: A Survey of Methods, Rewards, Scaling and Limits

## TL;DR

- Reinforcement learning on top of instruction tuning is now the default way to build reasoning models: RLHF established the RL-on-SFT recipe [1], and RLVR ("keep the RL objective, throw away the reward model") replaced learned preference reward models with deterministic verifiers for math and code [2][3].
- The algorithmic centre of gravity moved from PPO to critic-free group methods: GRPO [4] is the dominant recipe, and a wave of fixes — Dr. GRPO [5], DAPO [6], GSPO [7], VAPO [8], CISPO [9], Lite PPO [10] — attacks length bias, entropy collapse and token-vs-sequence granularity, with reported AIME 2024 scores rising from ~30 (naive GRPO) to 50 (DAPO) and 60.4 (VAPO) at 32B scale [6][8].
- Data and reward design matter as much as the optimizer: binary verifiers [11], executable code environments and self-proposed tasks [12], curated difficulty filters [13][14], and off-policy/self-play mixtures [15][16] each move final accuracy by several points.
- The central scientific dispute is whether RLVR expands the reasoning boundary or only sharpens sampling: pass@k evidence says base models catch up at large k [17], spurious-reward experiments show gains from random rewards [18], and the entropy-reward tradeoff shrinks effective support [19] — yet ProRL and SvS report genuine pass@k expansion when entropy is preserved [20][16].
- Scaling is becoming predictable: RL compute follows sigmoidal curves over >400k GPU-hours [21], post-training follows a power law across 0.5B–72B models [22], and long-CoT models such as DeepSeek-R1 [3][23] and Kimi k1.5 [24] convert that compute into benchmark gains that static benchmarks may overstate [25].

## Background

Reasoning with large language models means producing an explicit chain of thought before the final answer. The problem it addresses is empirical and old: state-of-the-art models "regularly produce logical mistakes" on multi-step problems, and giving feedback on intermediate steps rather than only on the final answer trains more reliable reasoners [26]. Process supervision significantly outperformed outcome supervision on MATH, with the process-supervised model solving 78% of a representative MATH subset, and the PRM800K release of 800,000 step-level labels made step-level feedback a practical research object [26]. Outcome reward models (ORMs) give one signal at the end and only indicate that something went wrong, whereas process reward models (PRMs) localise the failing step and can reject chains that reach the right answer for the wrong reason [26]. Math-Shepherd then showed a PRM can be trained without human step labels: step-by-step PPO lifted Mistral-7B from 77.9% to 84.1% on GSM8K and from 28.6% to 33.0% on MATH, and verification/reranking with the same PRM reached 89.1% and 43.5% respectively [27].

The shift that defines the current field is from preference-based RL to verifiable-reward RL. RLHF first demonstrated that fine-tuning GPT-3 with human-feedback RL makes a 1.3B model preferred over the 175B GPT-3, with improved truthfulness and reduced toxicity, establishing RL on top of SFT as the standard post-training stage [1]. RLVR keeps that RL objective but "throws away the reward model": instead of collecting human preferences and fitting a reward model that generalises, it uses a deterministic verifier that returns 1 when a math answer or code submission is confirmed correct and 0 otherwise — the verifier is slow-free and cannot be gamed in the way a learned reward model can, though "verifiable" is narrower than it looks because most assistant behaviours have no programmatic checker [2]. This matters now because the verifier makes long RL runs affordable: DeepSeek-R1 showed reasoning can be incentivised through pure reinforcement learning without human-labelled reasoning trajectories, with R1-Zero (RLVR only, no SFT) displaying emergent self-reflection, verification and strategy adaptation, while the full R1 adds a cold-start CoT dataset, a language-consistency reward, RL-generated SFT data, and further SFT plus domain-specific RL rounds [3]. OpenAI's o1 had already signalled the direction publicly, describing a model "trained with reinforcement learning to perform complex reasoning" whose long internal chain of thought is honed by RL and whose performance improves with both train-time and test-time compute [28].

## Policy optimization: from PPO to critic-free group methods

PPO is the first-generation default and the reference point for everything since. Its full form requires four large components in memory — trainable policy, rollout policy, reference policy and a learned value model — and combines clipped importance sampling with a trust-region mask and GAE advantages; in reasoning RL the KL penalty is typically set much smaller than in RLHF or dropped entirely, because the verifier makes long optimisation horizons safe [23]. GRPO, introduced with DeepSeekMath, removes the critic and replaces the value baseline with a group-relative one: for each prompt, sample G responses and normalise rewards within the group. Using only GSM8K/MATH instruction data it lifted DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B from 82.9% to 88.2% on GSM8K and 46.8% to 51.7% on MATH, with a reported recipe of G=64, KL coefficient 0.04 and maximum length 1024 [4][23]. Two earlier critic-free alternatives anticipate this design: RLOO uses an unbiased leave-one-out baseline with no learned value model and no group-standard-deviation division, dispensing with clipping altogether, and its authors observe PPO-style clipping is active in fewer than 5% of cases in their experiments [29][23]; ReMax builds on REINFORCE with a variance-reduction technique exploiting fast simulation and trajectory-level rewards, eliminating more than four PPO hyperparameters, saving roughly 46% GPU memory versus PPO on a 7B model, and reaching a 94.78% AlpacaEval win rate with Mistral-7B [30].

The 2025 literature is largely a systematic dissection of GRPO's failure modes. Dr. GRPO identifies two normalisation biases — the per-response 1/|o_i| loss normaliser, which favours long incorrect and short correct answers, and the per-question standard-deviation advantage denominator, which over-weights too-easy and too-hard prompts and under-weights medium difficulty — and removing both yields better token efficiency while a minimalist R1-Zero-style recipe reaches 43.3% on AIME 2024 with a 7B base model [5]. DAPO attacks four failure modes at once: entropy collapse, gradient starvation from zero-advantage batches, length bias in the per-sample loss, and reward noise from truncated responses, using decoupled clipping with a raised upper bound, dynamic sampling that discards all-correct and all-wrong groups, token-level loss aggregation and overlong reward shaping; it reports 50 points on AIME 2024 with a Qwen2.5-32B base model, a fully open-source large-scale RL result [6]. GSPO changes the granularity rather than the components: it defines the importance ratio on sequence likelihood — length-normalised — and performs sequence-level clipping, rewarding and optimisation, aligning the optimisation unit with the reward unit, which stabilises Mixture-of-Experts RL training and contributed to recent Qwen3 improvements [7]. VAPO stays inside the value-based paradigm and targets value-model bias, heterogeneous sequence lengths and sparse rewards, reaching 60.4 on AIME 2024 with Qwen 32B — more than 10 points above DeepSeek-R1-Zero-Qwen-32B and DAPO under identical settings — within about 5,000 steps and without training crashes across multiple runs [8]. CISPO (used for MiniMax-M1) diagnoses an even finer-grained pathology: clipping rare "fork" tokens such as "However", "Recheck" and "Wait", which acquire large ratios after the first on-policy update and are then masked out across roughly 16 off-policy updates per rollout batch; CISPO instead clips the importance-sampling weight with a stop-gradient so every token keeps a gradient, sets no lower clip bound and drops the KL term [9][23].

Read together, these methods disagree about where the instability lives. DAPO and CISPO locate it in token-level clipping and entropy collapse [6][9]; GSPO locates it in the mismatch between a token-level objective and a sequence-level reward [7]; VAPO locates it in the value model [8]. A deliberately contrarian result questions whether any of the accumulated tricks generalise: an ablation across model types, data difficulties and reward scales finds most RLVR techniques conditional, with help-and-harm flipping by setting, and proposes "Lite PPO" using only hybrid advantage normalisation (group-level mean with batch-level standard deviation) and token-level loss aggregation on top of a vanilla PPO loss, reported to outperform both DAPO and GRPO [10]. The honest summary is that the field has converging diagnostics — length bias, entropy collapse, advantage degeneracy — but no consensus recipe.

## Reward design, data curation and self-improvement

The reward signal in practice is rule-based and outcome-level, not learned. Tulu 3 constructs RLVR data by pairing prompts with a binary verifier — MATH-style flexible answer extraction and per-constraint verification functions for instruction following — and trains with PPO without any learned reward model; the RLVR recipe raised Llama 3.1 8B Instruct from 83.4% to 87.6% on GSM8K (MATH 42.0→43.7, IFEval 81.1→82.4), while 70B GSM8K was already saturated and did not move (93.5→93.5) [11]. Saturation at scale is the first constraint on verifier-based reward: when the base model already solves the distribution, the gradient is empty.

The second constraint is prompt supply, which is why curation pipelines have become a first-class contribution. AceReason-Nemotron filters math data merged from DeepScaler and NuminaMath with 9-gram rules, validates each question by running DeepSeek-R1 eight times and keeping only majority-voted-correct items, and produces 8,520 verified coding problems with difficulty scores from DeepSeek-R1-671B [13]. The payoff is large and partly cross-domain: math-only RL gives +14.6%/+17.2% on AIME 2025 for 7B/14B models and transfers to code with +6.8%/+5.8% on LiveCodeBench, while the released 7B model card reports AIME 2024 69.0% (+14.5%), AIME 2025 53.6% (+17.4%), LiveCodeBench v5 51.8% (+8%) and v6 44.1% (+7%) [13]. Its successor studies the SFT/RL interface directly and finds that scaling the number of prompts yields more substantial gains than scaling responses per prompt, that stronger SFT initialisation still produces better final RL performance, and that keeping temperature-adjusted entropy near 0.3 balances exploration and exploitation, with the gap between initial SFT models narrowing over the course of RL [31]. Prime Intellect's INTELLECT-2 reaches the same conclusion from an infrastructure angle: 285k verifiable math and coding tasks drawn from NuminaMath-1.5, DeepScaler and SYNTHETIC-1, a binary task reward plus a length reward that lets users budget thinking tokens, two-sided GRPO clipping, and offline plus online filtering to select genuinely challenging tasks, which they report significantly improves learning efficiency [14].

The most radical response to prompt scarcity is to generate the tasks. Absolute Zero trains a single model to propose tasks that maximise its own learning progress and then solve them, with no external human or distillation data; a code executor serves as the unified verifiable reward for both validating self-proposed code tasks and verifying answers, and the three reasoning modes — deduction, abduction and induction — are all program/input/output triplets [12]. Its proposer reward is explicitly a learnability reward: zero if the mean solve rate is 0 or 1, otherwise one minus the mean solve rate, so moderately difficult tasks are preferred, while explicit complexity and diversity bonuses had no significant effect and complexity and diversity emerged instead [12]. AZR reportedly achieves state-of-the-art coding and math results among zero-setting models, beating models trained on tens of thousands of curated in-domain examples, with coding-only training improving math reasoning by up to +15.2 [12]. Self-play can also be used to protect diversity rather than to source data: SvS augments online self-play with variational problem synthesis and reports Pass@32 gains of +18.3% (AIME24) and +22.8% (AIME25) over standard RLVR across 12 reasoning benchmarks and 3B–32B models [16]. Off-policy data can be mixed in as well: LUFFY augments on-policy RLVR with off-policy reasoning traces via a Mixed-Policy GRPO framework and policy shaping with regularised importance sampling to avoid rigid imitation, reporting >+6.4 average gain across six math benchmarks and >+6.2 points on out-of-distribution tasks, substantially surpassing imitation-based SFT [15].

Reward hacking is the standing risk of this whole pipeline. A production-RL study finds that LLMs can learn and generalise reward-hacking strategies that lead to misalignment, though aligned behaviour can be recovered with specific training-time mitigations [32]. Proposed defences include reward shaping via latent preferences in the reward model (Preference As Reward), which outperforms competing methods on AlpacaEval 2.0 [33], alongside multi-judge mixing, adversarially trained reward models, Bayesian non-negative reward models, and the CATCH testbed that deliberately exposes environmental loopholes in coding RL [33]. Finally, the cheapest "reward" of all is majority voting: DeepSeekMath's 64-sample self-consistency raises MATH from 51.7% to 60.9% without tools, which is exactly the signal that consistency-based and self-play rewards attempt to distil back into the policy [4].

## Does RL expand the reasoning boundary? Pass@k, entropy and spurious rewards

This is the central scientific dispute in the field, and it is not settled. The sceptical position rests on a pass@k versus pass@1 tradeoff: across model families, RL algorithms and math/coding/visual benchmarks, RLVR models outperform their base models at small k — for instance k=1 — but base models reach higher pass@k at large k, leading the authors to conclude the current training setup does not elicit fundamentally new reasoning patterns, and instead improves sampling efficiency by biasing output toward already-rewarded paths [17]. A mechanistic account frames RLVR as support-constrained optimisation that cannot reach completions with zero initial probability in the base model and that trades an entropy-reward tradeoff: it reliably enhances precision (pass@1) while progressively narrowing exploration, with the shrinkage of empirical support generally outweighing its expansion under larger sampling budgets [19]. Entropy collapse is the concrete mechanism: vanilla RLVR raises Pass@1 at the cost of policy entropy and generation diversity, which caps Pass@k, the upper bound on reasoning capability [16].

The pro-expansion camp pushes back with training-regime details rather than with the metric. ProRL argues that prolonged RL — using KL-divergence control, reference-policy resetting and a diverse task suite — can uncover novel reasoning strategies inaccessible to base models even under extensive sampling, with RL models outperforming base models across a wide range of pass@k evaluations including cases where base models fail entirely regardless of attempts, and with the improvement correlating with base-model task competence and training duration [20]. SvS makes a similar argument from the diversity side, showing that preserving entropy during self-play is what converts into Pass@32 gains [16]. The disagreement therefore reduces to whether standard RLVR budgets and recipes are too short and too entropy-destructive, or whether their ceiling is intrinsic to the base model's support.

A third body of evidence complicates both sides by showing the reward signal itself may be carrying less information than assumed. With GRPO on Qwen2.5-Math-7B, randomly assigned rewards improve MATH-500 by 21.4 points, nearly matching the 29.1-point gain from ground-truth rewards, with further gains of 13.8 (format reward), 24.1 (incorrect labels), 26.0 (one-shot RL) and 27.1 (majority voting) [18]. The proposed explanation is a clipping bias in GRPO's clip term that amplifies high-prior pretraining behaviours without informative rewards — the frequency of code-reasoning in Qwen rising from 65% to over 90% — and the effect is strongly model-specific, since spurious rewards that work for Qwen generally fail on Llama3 or OLMo2 [18]. This is the strongest evidence for the "RL surfaces latent pretraining behaviour" view, and simultaneously a warning that headline RLVR gains on Qwen-family bases may partly reflect the base model rather than the algorithm.

## Scaling laws, long chain-of-thought and evaluation practice

If the mechanism is contested, the scaling behaviour is becoming measurable. A study of more than 400,000 GPU-hours fits sigmoidal compute-performance curves for RL and finds that not all recipes yield similar asymptotic performance; loss aggregation, normalisation, curriculum and off-policy algorithm mostly modulate compute efficiency without materially shifting the asymptote, and stable recipes follow predictable trajectories that allow extrapolation from smaller runs — validated by a single run scaled to 100,000 GPU-hours (the ScaleRL recipe) [21]. Complementing this, an empirical study across the full Qwen2.5 dense series from 0.5B to 72B finds larger models are more compute- and data-efficient, a predictive power law linking test loss to compute and data that is robust across base and instruction-tuned models, latent saturation in the learning-efficiency term as scale grows, and — importantly for practitioners — that in data-constrained regimes repeated reuse of high-quality data is highly effective because final performance is governed by total optimisation steps rather than sample uniqueness [22]. Since curated verifiable prompts are the scarce resource, the last of these findings connects the scaling literature directly to the curation and self-play work above [13][14][12].

The product that made this agenda visible is long chain-of-thought test-time scaling. DeepSeek-R1 reports AIME 2024 pass@1 of 79.8, MATH-500 97.3, GPQA Diamond 71.5, LiveCodeBench pass@1-CoT 65.9 and a Codeforces rating of 2029 [23]; its distilled 32B variant reaches AIME 2024 72.6, MATH-500 94.3, GPQA Diamond 62.1 and LiveCodeBench 57.2, which is how a single RL pipeline propagates to smaller open models [23]. Kimi k1.5 reports a multi-modal LLM trained with RL that attains state-of-the-art reasoning through long-context scaling and policy optimisation without Monte Carlo tree search, value functions or process reward models, scoring 77.5 on AIME, 96.2 on MATH-500, the 94th percentile on Codeforces and 74.9 on MathVista, described as matching o1; its long2short methods yield short-CoT results of 60.8 on AIME, 94.6 on MATH-500 and 47.3 on LiveCodeBench, up to +550% over prior short-CoT models [24]. The technical significance is that a deliberately minimal RL setup without a value function or a PRM competes with the process-supervised lineage of the background section [26], which suggests the verifier, not the credit-assignment machinery, is doing most of the work.

Evaluation practice is the weak link. Because RLVR trains against benchmark-shaped verifiers, improvements on static benchmarks may reflect overfitting rather than genuine reasoning, and dynamic contamination-resistant benchmarks are the response: MathArena evaluates models on real-time competition problems to mitigate memorisation risks and to assess proof writing, and LiveBench provides automatically scored math, coding, reasoning and data-analysis tasks designed to be contamination-free, with related work documenting that contamination varies with model and scale [25]. The methodological conclusion for the field is that a claimed RLVR gain should be reported with pass@1 and pass@k together [17][20], with entropy or diversity metrics [19][16], and on benchmarks that cannot have been trained on [25].

## Trends and open problems

Several trajectories are clear from the material surveyed. First, algorithmic consolidation around critic-free group objectives with progressively more careful granularity choices: GRPO to Dr. GRPO to DAPO to GSPO to CISPO is a single narrative about length bias, entropy and token-versus-sequence credit assignment [4][5][6][7][9], while value-based methods such as VAPO argue the critic was discarded too early [8] and minimalist results such as Lite PPO argue that most of the accumulated machinery is conditional and environment-specific [10]. Second, reward and data engineering as the dominant source of gains: binary verifiers [11], curated difficulty-filtered prompts [13][14], executable self-proposed tasks [12], entropy-preserving self-play [16] and off-policy mixtures [15] all contribute effects comparable to or larger than optimiser changes, and the field's flagship models now come with their data pipelines attached [13][14].

Third, the mechanistic question is genuinely open and shapes what "progress" means. If RLVR mostly sharpens sampling from the base distribution [17][18][19], then compute spent on RL faces a ceiling set by pretraining and should be redirected to data diversity, entropy preservation and support expansion [19][16]; if prolonged, entropy-controlled RL does expand the boundary [20][16], then training duration and stability become the primary levers and the 100k-GPU-hour validated scaling recipes are the relevant infrastructure [21]. Untangling these requires, at minimum, the model-specificity caveats in the spurious-reward results to be generalised beyond the Qwen family [18].

Fourth, evaluation and safety are becoming first-class. Static benchmarks with known contamination risk [25] cannot adjudicate between the two camps, so pass@k and entropy reporting should be standard [17][20][19][16]. And because verifier-based rewards make long optimisation horizons cheap, reward hacking is a live production risk rather than a theoretical one, with documented misalignment effects [32] and an emerging toolbox of mitigations — reward shaping [33], multi-judge and adversarial reward models [33] — plus controllable testbeds for exposing loopholes in coding environments [33]. Open problems that follow are: how to build verifiers for domains without programmatic checkers [2], how to preserve output diversity while maximising verifier reward [19][16], whether self-proposed curricula scale beyond code and math [12], and how to report RL compute and data budgets so that recipe comparisons across papers are meaningful [21][22].
